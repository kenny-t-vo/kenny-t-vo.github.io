kenny vo. writings.

a city in film.

11 september 2026.

on berlin.

This summer I took a study abroad course to Berlin led by Peter Sealy. These are a few photographs and reflections on the city’s history, architecture, and culture, worked through analog film making and photography.

Berlin watches and hates being watched. Police move through the streets in threes and nobody looks up from their phone. Raise a camera and the air shifts, three faces turning at once to find the lens. The city keeps the files its secret police wrote on its own citizens, open to anyone who books a tour, and still the offense that registers is the stranger pointing something in your vicinity. Watched into paranoia once, it has settled on terms: the state may look, a person may not.

People tell you about the grey. You arrive expecting concrete and flat hard weather, and in places that is what you get, but August held and the sun stayed on the face of every building until nearly ten, and the parks filled with people lying in it as though it were owed to them. Whatever I thought this city would be, I had the exposure off.

It keeps its wounds where you pass them. Brass squares set into the pavement outside the last apartment somebody chose for themselves. Two rows of cobbles across an intersection where cars now park. A pane of glass to frame some empty shelves in the ground. None of these beg for a trip. You cross them on the way to lunch and the day carries on, which is either what the approach achieves or what it costs.

I kept thinking the scars were sacred and then remembering that somebody designed them. A memorial is a commission like anything else. Someone chose the typeface and the stone, someone costed it, a jury picked it over the others. Although that is not a debunking, it is what makes standing inside the memory strange, because knowing how the grief was built does not make it less grief.

I took photos I liked of buildings I should not like. Good light does not check the history of what it lands on, and some of those frames came back beautiful in a way I cannot separate from what was done behind the facade. The discomfort may be the only honest way to hold a camera here.

Berlin loves a surface, which is not the insult it sounds like. It loved the clean plane of Bauhaus in the twenties, it loves its parks and its cobbles and its long avenues, and it loves them enough to build them a second time.

A palace front has gone back up from old photographs and private donations, wrapped around a new museum, on the ground where the last republic’s own palace was pulled down for being the wrong kind of history. It would be easy to file this under nostalgia. Nearer the truth is that the city knows old beauty is useful, having watched a regime put it to use once already.

As a visitor it is hard not to reach for the ghosts first. Nobody is flying the old insignia; that argument is settled for the most part and is policed. The softer attachment underneath, the fondness for a beautiful history, because it arrives looking like taste rather than politics.

None of which explains why the beer costs less than the water.

I have not had this much fun in so long. Some of it belongs to the course, to the teaching and the architects and filmmakers who gave up their afternoons, and to the classmates, some old friends, some new, who walked every kilometre of it beside me.

Most of it was more ordinary than that. Lying in the park with nothing to do. Beers from the corner shop, drunk on the way to somewhere else. Pizza and vodka out of a juice box on the bridge, our legs through the railing next to everyone else’s. Getting turned away at a door and laughing about it the whole way to the next one. Walks long enough that the edges of the day started to grey out. I smiled through all of it in a way I had almost forgotten I could.

What I keep turning over is how easily the two sat together. You dance until the light comes back in a room the state built to broadcast propaganda at its own citizens, or in a bunker poured in the middle of the war. You lie on grass that is flat and open because a regime laid it out as an airfield while making plans for the rest of the map. On a Saturday a stadium built for that regime’s games fills up and sings for ninety minutes. Nobody near me was working through any of this. Not denial so much as the arithmetic of living somewhere. The buildings outlasted their purposes. People still need somewhere to be on a Friday.

The easy move is to list. Concrete and green, old and new, punk and imperial. I wrote that list but do not trust it anymore. Pairs are what you reach for when you have been somewhere three weeks and need the place to hold still long enough to be described.

What I did not expect was how much got handed over. Architects who cooked for us and sat down to eat with us. People who opened their homes to students they would never meet again. Filmmakers who put their own cameras in our hands, showed us how to load them, and let us walk out into their city with them.

Berlin spent three weeks refusing to be looked at, and its people kept handing me things to look with.
