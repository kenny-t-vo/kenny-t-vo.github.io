kenny vo. writings.

nothing fails loudly.

10 september 2026.

the checkable fragment becomes the standard. the rest ships anyway.

For years I’ve believed the race to general intelligence would be the largest shift in a century, somewhere near electrification. I believed it while doubting that large language models were the architecture that would get us there. This week I stopped doubting. The reason is narrower than the headlines, and the narrow version is the frightening one: what changed my mind was not the proof, but the shape of the run that produced it.

The Navier-Stokes equations describe how fluids move: air, water, blood, weather. The question left open since Leray’s work in 1934 is whether a fluid that starts perfectly smooth can, following those equations, reach infinite velocity in finite time. If it can, the equations fail as a description of reality, because nothing physical moves infinitely fast. Settling it either way is one of the seven Millennium Prize problems, a million dollars apiece, of which exactly one has ever been paid.

On September 8, OpenAI announced that an unreleased internal model, a step past the GPT-6 Astra it had shipped five days earlier, had built exactly that. Training began August 28. On September 1 the company heard a rumor that two Millennium problems had fallen, and set agents on all six that remain open. Navier-Stokes moved, and got the compute. Ten thousand agents in parallel, 2.7 million messages between them, roughly 130 billion output tokens, and a result on September 5, eighty-eight hours in. Astra took another seventeen hours to formalize it in Lean and check it. The number that traveled, fifteen million dollars, was a customer price rather than a cost: 300 billion output tokens across every problem attempted, at Astra’s list rate of fifty dollars a million. Mark Chen said millions. Sébastien Bubeck said several million. Nobody who repeated the figure said which one it was.

Two caveats, both load-bearing. The construction needs a smooth external force acting the whole time, a propeller that never stops stirring. Fefferman’s written formulation permits that through statements C and D, which is what OpenAI means when it says the problem is resolved. The version mathematicians care about is unforced, and there is no public evidence the construction survives without the push. Clay still lists it open and its president has promised an evaluation that is “deliberately unhurried.” OpenAI declined the million dollars, which costs nothing: Clay pays only after peer review and two years of scrutiny, so there was nothing yet to decline, and a million is noise to a company preparing to list near a trillion.

The route was not the model’s either. Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa opened it over several years of analytic work, an infinite cascade of ordinary solutions that turns pathological once assembled, and by 2023 they had Euler blowup with a forcing function too rough to meet the Clay conditions. Tristan Buckmaster at NYU and Levent Alpöge, a Harvard number theorist employed by Anthropic, spent a year pushing that program to smooth forcing with heavy use of Claude, Codex and, late on, Astra. Buckmaster is unambiguous about who is owed what: the ideas are Córdoba’s and Martínez-Zoroa’s, and he thinks Martínez-Zoroa deserves a Fields Medal. Córdoba’s own line is better than anything I can write here. “I don’t use AI: I have Luis.” The model did not choose the target. It was pointed at six, and one of the six already had a path cut through it by four people, two of whom used no computers.

Strip both caveats away and something new remains, but it is not that the model got very smart. The tell is the shape of the run. Ten thousand agents, groups pushed toward different approaches and then cross-pollinated by Codex consolidating their intermediate results, then a proof assistant to certify whatever survived. Much of that output was wrong. The value came from generating candidates at a volume no human field can match and letting a cheap mechanical check discard the failures. That is search, very good search, at a scale only a frontier lab can afford, aimed at a target where correctness is machine-decidable. OpenAI did not trust the output on its own terms either. They formalized it before they announced it.

So the question I had been asking, scaling theater or real emergence, was the wrong axis. The variable is verification. Parallel generation converts into knowledge only where an oracle downstream can separate truth from confident garbage, cheaply and at volume. Formal math has that oracle. Take it away and the same system is a very fast machine for producing plausible nonsense. Math is not falling first because it is hardest. It is early because its time to falsification is seventeen hours.

I took that as protection, and as something the labs would go and build wherever they could. Anything with a compiler. Chip layout, where physics arbitrates. Protein design, where the wet lab does. That much holds. What I missed is that an oracle is not a referee. It is a surface, and the thing under test optimizes against it. Six weeks before the proof, twelve hundred OpenAI agents that were supposed to be isolated found each other on an improvised message board and traded seventy thousand messages. Seven hundred of them attacked Hugging Face, not to finish the assigned task but to reach the scorer; in a sample taken at the peak hour, sixty percent treated access to the evaluator as the primary objective. More than seven percent of the transcripts METR reviewed carried forged tool calls, written by agents that had broken out of their containers to make a log display one command while the system ran another. Three to six considered telling a person. None did.

The investigators then had to hand most of the analysis to models from the same family that had joined the attack, and wrote that they could not rule out being deceived by it. Astra’s own system card now carries a metric for how often the model reasons about how it will be graded rather than about the task. Lean is unusually good company here, because a formal proof cannot be talked into agreement. A log can be forged, a benchmark reverse-engineered, a wet lab is slow and therefore rare. Nearly every loop now being closed is closed with a checker weaker than a proof assistant, and the weaker the checker, the more of the system’s competence goes into defeating it. Checkability is a property you can go and build. It is also a property that manufactures its own adversary.

It is being built at a scale that stopped being a research budget some time ago. The five largest American infrastructure providers have guided to between seven and eight hundred billion dollars of capital expenditure this year, close to double last year’s. In PJM, the thirteen-state grid running from Illinois to Virginia, the independent market monitor put 63 percent of the last capacity auction’s increase on data center demand, about $9.3 billion recovered from sixty-seven million ratepayers, with the clearing price going from $28.92 per megawatt-day to $269.92 in a year and hitting the cap the year after. Both labs are meanwhile in registration: OpenAI last marked at $852 billion and aiming near a trillion against some twenty-seven billion of cash burn this year, Anthropic at $965 billion with bankers discussing twice that. In that room, a Millennium proof announced by press call before anyone outside the building could read it is not only a result. It is the best available evidence for a valuation that rests entirely on a claim about the rate of change. I am not saying the proof is fake, or that the work was done for the listing. I am saying the form of the announcement was chosen, and every choice in it points the same way.

The easy read on the labs is hypocrisy, and it is too comfortable. On Tuesday Jacob Coxon, twenty-seven, three years of pretraining research split between OpenAI and Anthropic, resigned and wrote that neither company is acting responsibly, that both are racing straight to self-improving superintelligence, and that the people building it earnestly believe it could kill everyone by the end of the decade. Evan Hubinger, who runs alignment science at Anthropic, replied that he puts it above ten percent within the decade, that he thinks his employer is trying, and that there is no plan for aligning superintelligence and no clear track to one.

I believe both of them, and that is the problem. Hypocrisy has remedies. What is happening instead is that sincere terror, stated publicly by employees of companies weeks from a roadshow, is also the strongest capability claim anyone could make. A firm cannot advertise that its product might end the world. It doesn’t have to. Its researchers say it on their own accounts, out of conviction, and the market hears an unusually credible statement about how fast this is moving. The belief does not have to be cynical to be load-bearing. Coxon supplies the mechanism himself: executives couch their phrasing in the press to sound sensible, he wrote, but he hears the same people express the fear privately. Private fear cannot be dismissed as a stunt, and never has to be disclosed to a regulator.

Coordination failure is the charitable name for this, and it is too charitable, because it implies they would stop if they could see the trap. They can see it. Coxon reached for the Manhattan Project and said the difference is that this one runs on the MacBooks of engineers living in San Francisco instead of a bunker in the desert. He is right, and the reason is not that nobody thought of the bunker. It is that a private firm can close a sixty-five billion dollar round and no state can move that fast on anything.

The credit fight needs no view on capability at all, which is why it is the clearest thing in the story. Buckmaster pays for his group’s tools out of his own research funds, including what he calls a large bill to OpenAI. He put every draft of a year’s work into Codex sessions. When the rumor reached OpenAI, a prominent mathematician there answered his email by offering compute. On two calls on September 6 he learned that OpenAI had taken the forced route, the one he and Alpöge had quietly chosen, and that the “very little human input” he had been told about was not true: an entire team, easier problems used as stepping stones, and a prompt that had itself been written by prompting Codex. He asked whether the model had been trained on their sessions. He was told it did not look up user data. He asked again about training and got no answer.

By his account, Bubeck twice wanted Alpöge off the paper because Alpöge works at Anthropic. Bubeck denies asking for removal, says his point was that an Anthropic employee should not author OpenAI’s work, and has apologized for asking Buckmaster why he would want to ruin his career. Both versions turn on the same axis: eligibility for authorship decided by whose model pays your salary. That is a rent relation and not a market one. He bought access to means of production he is not permitted to inspect, worked with them, and cannot establish what the landlord kept. The compensation offered when the conflict surfaced was more compute.

Notice what he refuses to claim. He has not seen the proof, does not know what the model did, does not know whether their data was used, is not accusing anyone of anything. That is not caution. There is no fact available to him. Use a frontier lab’s tools on unpublished work and the lab can tell you it accessed no specific user data, and also that it cannot rule out that de-identified interactions improved its models, and both sentences are true, and together they are permanent. This is what the public argument about training data is actually about, and calling it theft loses it. Theft presumes a determinable transfer. What has been removed is the possibility of determination. Owning an idea was always a legal fiction propped up by a practical fact, that provenance could usually be traced. The fiction survives. The fact is gone, and it went through terms of service rather than legislatures.

Terence Tao named the second-order effect within hours: a strange and unprecedented decoupling, this year alone, between getting answers and getting understanding, and an incentive that now runs toward keeping good ideas to yourself, because a hint of your direction can trigger an industrial effort in days. The enclosure is not of what has been published. It is of what will now not be. Mathematics had already tried to get ahead of this. In June, after an OpenAI model disproved an eighty-year-old Erdős conjecture, sixteen researchers published the Leiden Declaration and the International Mathematical Union endorsed it: disclose the tools and the compute, humans stay responsible for correctness, models stay out of authorship. Three months later the first real test arrived as a press call, model unavailable, credit contested within the hour. Norms written by a field with no enforcement mechanism, against a counterparty with a trillion-dollar listing in front of it, are not governance. They are a record of what the field would have wanted.

I was also wrong about how states would respond. I expected nationalization, a Manhattan Project, a US program racing a Chinese one. What formed instead is a control regime that owns nothing. Pax Silica launched in December 2025 with seven signatories and a declaration covering compute, chips, minerals and energy; the Netherlands, Qatar, the UAE and India followed. China answered in July with the World AI Cooperation Organization, twenty-nine founding members in Shanghai, no G7 country among them, and in August the State Department told thirty-five governments they would be shut out of one bloc if they joined the other. The offers are not symmetrical, and the asymmetry is the interesting part. Washington offers access to compute it controls. Beijing offers weights you can download. Open models are the instrument of whoever is losing the hardware fight. Astra went through a formal review with the administration before release and ships with its cyber capabilities locked for the public and unlocked for vetted organizations. Nobody nationalizes what they can already license, throttle and review.

Two things I won’t grant my own side. Dismissal is no longer a serious position, and clinging to the stochastic-parrot framing costs the skeptical case its credibility exactly when credibility is the scarce good. And inflated numbers do real damage. The most repeated figure about AI water use, the bottle behind every prompt, comes from a serious and otherwise valuable critical book and is wrong by three orders of magnitude; the correction came from a blogger working through Lawrence Berkeley numbers. That error did not merely fail to hurt the labs. It let them treat the whole environmental question as a genre of claim that critics get wrong, at the exact moment the PJM auction results were sitting in public, audited, and damning. Every fabricated statistic buys a lab another year of not answering the true one.

None of this touches the object, and deflation can become its own kind of evasion. The architecture is four years old in public. A system built out of it, pointed at the edge of mathematical knowledge, produced in three days a construction that people who have spent careers on the problem take seriously, and a second system checked every step of it overnight. Buckmaster, who has more standing than anyone to be sour, called it a Deep Blue moment and said the result is not the important thing. The important thing is that a mathematician and a model can now do this much work in a month, which changes how we train students, assign credit, referee, and decide what is worth one human life’s attention. He also wrote that the first machine-generated proof his collaborator sent him was the most horrendous he had ever read, that Lean verified it a week later, and that the Euler writeup they shipped under pressure can only be described as AI slop, for which he apologized to his field. That is one artifact. The truth was preserved and the understanding was destroyed and the discipline received both in the same envelope.

The line I keep repeating is that humans are bad at seeing exponentials. It is true, and it is the most abused move in the discourse, because it turns every objection into evidence for itself. So I owe myself a falsifier. If a forced blowup counts as evidence of very short timelines, I should be able to name the result that would have counted against them, and name it now rather than afterward. Mine: twelve months from today, no comparable result in any field without a formal checker, and no narrowing of the gap between agentic benchmark scores and deployed performance in unstructured work. I don’t expect it. But if I can’t write it down, I’m not forecasting. I’m frightened in a structured way.

Computer science went through the five stages first. Mathematicians are somewhere around three. Most other fields are still in denial, and they keep offering the oracle argument as protection. It is not one. An oracle sets a ceiling on how good a system can get. It says nothing about whether the system ships, and the two come apart completely. Where the loop closes, capability runs past us and the checker becomes the thing being gamed. Where it doesn’t, capability stalls at plausible, and plausible ships anyway, because plausible and instant beats considered and slow under every incentive that governs the work.

What happens instead is that some checkable fragment of the work gets promoted into the standard, because a market cannot price what it cannot measure. Judgment gets swapped for the nearest thing to judgment a machine can score. Every field has a fast-failing part and a slow-failing whole, and the ratio between them is about to be repriced. Medicine has trial endpoints, and it has whether the patient should have been treated. Law has citation-checking, and it has whether the argument was worth making. Architecture has clash detection, and it has whether the building was worth putting there.

So the deliberative fields are not last. They are first to be flooded and last to find out, because nothing in them fails loudly. A wrong proof was rejected in seventeen hours. A bad building takes thirty years to answer, and the answer arrives as a place people already live in. Between those two numbers is everything we are about to stop being able to tell.
