kenny vo. writings.

nothing fails loudly.

9 september 2026.

the ai argument turns on verification, and most work cannot check itself.

For years I’ve believed the race to general intelligence would be the largest shift in a century, somewhere near electrification. I believed it while doubting that language models were the architecture that would get us there. This week I stopped doubting. The reason is narrower than the headlines, and the narrow version is the frightening one.

The Navier-Stokes equations describe how fluids move: air, water, blood, weather. The open question, unresolved for ninety years, is whether a fluid that starts perfectly smooth can, following those equations, reach infinite velocity in finite time. If it can, the equations fail as a description of reality, because nothing physical moves infinitely fast. Settling it either way is one of the seven Millennium Prize problems, a million dollars apiece, one of the seven solved.

On September 8, OpenAI announced that an unreleased internal model, a significant step past the already wave-crashing GPT-6 Astra, had constructed exactly that. Ten thousand agents in parallel for eighty-eight hours, roughly one hundred and thirty billion output tokens across the week’s problems, something like twenty million dollars of compute at current rates. The proof was then formalized in Lean and machine-checked, another seventeen hours on Astra.

Two caveats. The first: the construction requires an external force pushing on the fluid the entire time, a propeller that never stops stirring. The prize’s written rules do permit that route, which is what OpenAI means when it says the result establishes statements C and D of the official formulation. The version mathematicians care about is the unforced one, where nothing pushes and the blowup comes from the fluid’s own dynamics. The Clay Institute has not accepted the result and still lists the problem as open. OpenAI declined the million dollars, which is the clearest available read on how they privately rate their own claim.

The second: the forced route was not the model’s idea. Tristan Buckmaster, an NYU mathematician, says he and Levent Alpöge had spent months on the forced version of the closely related Euler equations, on a line opened by two other mathematicians, and that this is not a direction you arrive at in a few days from the bare problem statement. OpenAI’s own writeup says its effort began on September 1 after hearing a rumor it later traced to them. The model did not choose the target. It was pointed at one. Autonomous problem selection is the capability that would justify panic, and nothing here tested it.

Strip both caveats away and something new remains, but it is not that the model got smart. The tell is the shape of the run. Ten thousand agents, one hundred and thirty billion tokens, then a proof assistant to certify whatever survived. Most of that output was wrong. The value came from generating candidates at a volume no human field can match and letting a cheap mechanical check discard the failures. That is search, very good search, at a scale only a frontier lab can afford, aimed at a target where correctness is machine-decidable. OpenAI did not trust the output on its own terms either. They formalized it before they announced it.

So the question I had been asking, scaling theater or real emergence, was the wrong axis. The variable is verification. Parallel generation converts into knowledge only where an oracle downstream can separate truth from confident garbage, cheaply and at volume. Formal math has that oracle. Take it away and the same system is a very fast machine for producing plausible nonsense. Math is first because math can check itself.

That reframing does not calm me down, because the set of domains with usable oracles is larger than it looks and is being deliberately grown. Anything with a compiler. Chip layout, where physics arbitrates. Protein design, where the wet lab does. Trading, where the market does. In each, the loop closes without a human in it, and closing loops is now the main engineering agenda. The bottleneck was never compute, and inference costs are falling anyway. The bottleneck is checkability, and checkability is a property you can go build.

The architecture of conversational LLMs is four years old. Every analogy I reach for, electricity, the internet, print, took generations to unfold, leaving institutions time to deform around them. What frontier models fumble now, spatial reasoning, sustained judgment across long context, they may not fumble in two months, and we have no principled way to bound that.

The people inside are not reassuring. Jacob Coxon resigned from Anthropic on Tuesday after three years of pretraining research split between it and OpenAI, saying publicly that neither company is acting responsibly, that both are racing toward self-improving superintelligence, and that people in these labs earnestly believe the technology could kill everyone by the end of the decade. Evan Hubinger, who runs alignment science at Anthropic, replied that he puts that outcome above ten percent within the next decade.

The easy read is hypocrisy: labs that promise to benefit humanity, hire ethicists, publish safety frameworks, then do whatever the valuation requires. That read is too comfortable, and Coxon’s account says something worse. He describes OpenAI as a place where many have not internalized the stakes, and Anthropic as a place where the stakes are understood and the race continues anyway, because nobody else will act responsibly, so it has to be us. That is a coordination failure among sincere people, and it is harder to fix than greed. Greed has known remedies. Prisoner’s dilemmas do not.

The credit fight is where the damage is already concrete, and it requires no view on capability at all. Buckmaster alleges OpenAI drew on his Codex sessions, that he was pressured to drop Alpöge’s credit, and that when he threatened to go public he was asked why he would want to ruin his career. Sébastien Bubeck disputes the framing and has retracted that last line. OpenAI says it accessed no user data to solve the problem, and that it cannot rule out that de-identified data from the pair’s usage improved its models. That second sentence is the whole structural fact, and it is permanent: use a frontier lab’s tools on unpublished work and the lab cannot tell you it did not see your unpublished work. Terence Tao named the deeper version, a decoupling this year between getting answers and getting understanding, and warned that the incentive now runs toward researchers keeping good ideas to themselves. Intellectual property is being renegotiated in real time, not by legislatures but by whoever owns the tools, and the first-order effect is less openness.

I was also wrong about how states would respond. I expected nationalization, a Manhattan Project, a US program racing a Chinese one. What formed instead is Pax Silica, launched in December 2025, a supply chain and export control bloc built around chokepoints, with China’s World AI Cooperation Organization as the counterweight. Washington worked out that it can control compute allocation and licensing without owning anything. Nobody nationalizes what they can already throttle.

Two things I won’t grant my own side. Dismissal is no longer a serious position, and clinging to the stochastic-parrot framing costs the skeptical case its credibility exactly when credibility is the scarce good. And inflated numbers do not help. The energy and water demands of compute are a real constraint with real local costs, and the viral versions of those figures are usually off by an order of magnitude. Every fabricated statistic buys a lab another year of not answering the true one.

The line I keep repeating is that humans are bad at seeing exponentials. It is true, and it is the most abused move in the discourse, because it turns every objection into evidence for itself. So I owe myself a falsifier. If a forced blowup counts as evidence of very short timelines, I should be able to name the result this year that would have counted against them. If I can’t, I’m not forecasting. I’m frightened in a structured way.

Computer science went through the five stages first. Mathematicians are somewhere around three. But I keep hearing the oracle argument offered as protection, and it is not one. An oracle sets a ceiling on how good a system can get. It says nothing about whether the system ships, and the two come apart completely. Where the loop closes, capability runs past us. Where it doesn’t, capability stalls at plausible, and plausible ships anyway, because plausible and instant beats considered and slow under every incentive that governs the work. The tools being built for design are not waiting for a proof checker.

What happens instead is that some checkable fragment of the work gets promoted into the standard, because a market cannot price what it cannot measure. Judgment gets swapped for the nearest thing to judgment a machine can score. So the deliberative fields, design among them, are not last. They are first to be flooded and last to find out, because nothing in them fails loudly. A wrong proof is rejected in seventeen hours. A bad building takes thirty years to say so, and by then nobody can prove what caused it.
